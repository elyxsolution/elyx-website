## Does My Business Need an App? Four Tests It Has to Pass

Your business needs an app only if it passes at least one of four tests, and the first is the hardest: customers must have a reason to open it every week. RNF Technologies sets out the four in its article "Most businesses do not need an app", as read on 17 November 2026.

1. **Frequency.** Reordering, booking, tracking, loyalty and account use pass, while an annual renewal or a one-off purchase fails. RNF says phones hold more than 80 apps but open about 9 a day, and 71% of users stop using an app within 90 days of installing it.
2. **Hardware or offline use.** The job needs the camera, GPS, Bluetooth, biometrics or offline storage in ways a browser handles badly. RNF names field service, in-store scanning and delivery routing. A menu, a brochure or a contact form does not qualify.
3. **Time-sensitive alerts.** You have something worth sending now, such as a delivery update. RNF puts push open rates near 80% against 20% for email, then warns that the same channel burns goodwill when it carries promotions nobody asked for.
4. **Money moves in the app.** Reorders, bookings or subscriptions happen there. RNF notes that the order-value gains brands report come from customers who already had a reason to install.

Innowise, an app developer, adds a social test: your customers already expect one, as with banking. My reading is that this rarely applies to a local Indian business, because its customers arrive through Google Maps, search and WhatsApp.

## A Decision Checklist You Can Copy

Answer the eight questions below, then apply the three gates; the questions come from RNF's tests, Innowise's signs and Kit Labs' signs, and the gates are my own rule, not a validated score. Copy the list into a note and answer yes or no.

- [ ] **1.** Do your top 100 customers each deal with you at least twelve times a year? (RNF says under twelve, stop and improve the website.)
- [ ] **2.** Does the job need hardware or offline use that a mobile browser handles badly?
- [ ] **3.** Can you write twelve genuinely useful notifications for the coming year?
- [ ] **4.** Will bookings, reorders or subscriptions happen inside the app?
- [ ] **5.** Are customers already asking for an easier way to book or reorder? (Kit Labs lists this as a sign.)
- [ ] **6.** Is year-two maintenance funded in writing? (Innowise and RNF both say apps need it.)
- [ ] **7.** Does your website already load fast on a phone and take enquiries by call, WhatsApp or form?
- [ ] **8.** Does payback fit inside 24 months on pessimistic inputs? (The worked example below shows how.)

**Gate 1:** at least one yes among questions 1 to 4. **Gate 2:** a yes to question 6. **Gate 3:** a yes to questions 7 and 8. Question 5 adds weight but never replaces a gate.

If every gate passes, scope one narrow first release. If any gate fails, put the money into the website, where RNF says nearly every app install starts anyway.

## The Middle Step: A Website That Installs Like an App

A progressive web app (PWA) is a website built to behave like an app, and it is the cheapest way to learn whether customers would use the real thing. RNF says it installs to the home screen from the browser, caches content for offline use and can send notifications, all without store review.

RNF's advice is to ship one first when frequency is plausible but unproven, then let install and return rates decide whether a native build is worth the money. Native still wins for deep hardware access, background processing and store-only in-app purchases.

This site's app page says a fast, installable-feeling website is often enough. Elyx does not list a separate PWA package, so ask for a written scope.

## Worked Example: Would an App Pay Back for a Gym?

On assumed numbers, an app for a 400-member gym fails RNF's 24-month test by about ₹2 lakh, and the arithmetic shows what would have to change. The gym is fictional and every input is an assumption, not a client or a quote. The method is RNF's four inputs: installable audience, install rate, retention after 90 days and value per retained user.

1. **Installable audience.** 400 members who already visit regularly.
2. **Installs.** An assumed 40% install: 400 × 0.40 = 160 people.
3. **Still active at 90 days.** RNF says 71% leave, so 29% stay: 160 × 0.29 = 46 people, rounded down from 46.4.
4. **Value.** An assumed extra ₹300 a month in gross profit per retained member: 46 × 300 × 24 months = ₹3.31 lakh.
5. **Cost.** An assumed ₹4 lakh build, plus 15 to 20 percent a year for two years: 4 × 0.15 × 2 = ₹1.2 lakh to 4 × 0.20 × 2 = ₹1.6 lakh, so ₹5.2 lakh to ₹5.6 lakh in total.

The app is short by ₹5.2 − 3.31 = ₹1.89 lakh to ₹5.6 − 3.31 = ₹2.29 lakh. To break even, each retained member would need to add ₹5.2 lakh ÷ (46 × 24 = 1,104 member-months) = about ₹471 a month at the low cost, or ₹5.6 lakh ÷ 1,104 = about ₹507 at the high one.

Replace each input with your own numbers, including the quote you receive for the build.

## When an App Genuinely Pays Off

An app pays off when it is part of how customers use you every week, not another way to read your website. The cases below come from Innowise, RNF and Kit Labs, and the business column is my reading of the frequency test.

| Business | Likely route | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Dental or medical clinic | Website with WhatsApp booking | Patients visit a few times a year |
| Coaching centre | Landing page; app only for daily practice | Admission enquiries are one-off |
| Restaurant or café | Website with Maps and menu | Diners decide at the moment of search |
| Gym or membership studio | App can pass the tests | Weekly check-ins and bookings |
| Delivery fleet or field team | App likely needed | Live tracking and GPS |

Innowise names three further cases: a product that is mobile by nature, such as fintech or e-learning; a loyalty model built on frequent interaction; and real-time bookings or courier tracking. Kit Labs adds internal tools: if staff lose hours on scheduling, tracking and follow-ups, an app can remove that manual work.

Where neither repeat use nor a device feature exists, Innowise lists the opposite signs: your website already does the job, customers interact rarely, there is no maintenance budget and your audience does not expect an app.

## When a Better Website Wins: The Indian Customer's Route

A better website wins when customers find you by searching, asking on WhatsApp or opening Maps, because an app cannot serve someone at the moment they search. RNF lists discovery-led demand as a reason not to build, since a person has to find and install the app before it helps.

Google's launch post for its Business Profile app says Google connects people to nearby businesses more than 9 billion times a month, including over 1 billion phone calls and 3 billion direction requests. The text I read carries no date and no India split, so treat it as scale, not a local figure.

My reading is that these routes are the ones Indian customers already use: a search, a Maps listing, a tap to call or a WhatsApp message. The Ashirwad Dental redesign on this site used exactly that: patients start a WhatsApp booking in one tap, and it took 8 weeks from kickoff to launch. For the options, see [adding WhatsApp booking to your website](/blog/whatsapp-booking-for-website/).

Fix these first, which is where the money does more than an app would:

- **Speed on a phone.** Many visitors are on patchy 4G, so compress images and keep pages lean.
- **One-tap contact.** A call button and WhatsApp click-to-chat on every page.
- **Maps and hours.** Accurate timings and directions, matched to your Google Business Profile.
- **Payment where you sell.** UPI and card payments on the site, if you take orders.

## FAQ

### Does a small business need a mobile app?

Most small businesses do not need their own app yet. RNF Technologies says to build one only when customers return at least weekly, or the job needs push notifications, offline access or device hardware. Otherwise a fast mobile website reaches more people for less.

### Would customers actually download a local business's app?

Only if they have a repeat reason to open it. RNF says phones hold more than 80 apps but open about 9 a day, and 71% of users stop using an app within 90 days. A shop or clinic visited a few times a year rarely passes that test.

### How much does an app cost to maintain each year?

RNF says to budget 15 to 20 percent of the build price every year, covering operating system updates, bug fixes and store compliance. On an assumed ₹4 lakh build that is ₹0.6 lakh to ₹0.8 lakh a year. It continues for as long as the app exists.

### What is a progressive web app?

A progressive web app is a website built to behave like an app. RNF says it installs from the browser, caches content for offline use and can send notifications without store review, so it is a cheaper way to test demand.

### Does Google My Business have an app?

Yes, Google launched a free app for owners to manage their Business Profile, which its launch post calls the Google My Business app. It lets owners post offers, reply to reviews and see profile results. Check the store for the current name and features.

### Do I need a company or licence to publish an app?

The research behind this page does not cover company registration or licence rules for publishing an app. Ask a chartered accountant or company secretary, and read Apple's and Google's developer terms. Rules change, so confirm before you pay for a build.

### Can I make an app for my business for free?

No-code builders exist, and Salesforce says they can launch an app in weeks or days. This page did not test or price any of them. Store fees, upkeep and the time to keep it current stay, so free tools still carry a cost.
