Website maintenance services are the recurring jobs that keep a live site online, patched, backed up and correct: software updates, backups, security scans, uptime and form monitoring, speed checks and small content edits. Skipping them rarely breaks a site on day one. The damage is quiet, and the usual way owners find out is that a customer tells them or the site is already compromised. This page rests on five Indian providers' own pages, read on 20 November 2026, so every figure below is a vendor's claim, not independent data.

- **Six jobs.** Updates, backups, monitoring, security, speed and content changes are what the providers list, in different words.
- **Check intervals.** A failure lasts as long as the gap between checks, and the worked example below shows the arithmetic.
- **Failure stories.** None of the five pages gives a named, verifiable case, so this page says what is documented and labels the rest as my reading.
- **Plans.** One provider's four plans differ mainly in edits, backup frequency and response time, not in the word "maintenance".

## Updates: Why Old Code Is the Way In

Updates matter because outdated software is the easiest way into a website, and the same updates can also break it if they go straight to the live site. Aarav Infotech says old code is the easiest way attackers get in, and that any one update can break a checkout, a form or a layout.

Its answer is to apply core, theme and plugin updates weekly, test them on a staging copy first and check the main buyer journeys before going live. Skynet Technologies adds housekeeping: deleting unused plugins and themes, clearing logs and junk files, and optimising the database.

A plugin is an add-on that gives a site one feature, such as a form or a payment button. Each one is another piece of software that needs patching, which is why the number installed matters as much as how often they are updated. The studio's care page describes the same safeguard: dependency updates are tested on a staging copy before they touch the live site.

## Backups: Only as Good as the Last Restore

A backup protects you only if a recent copy exists away from the server and someone has proved it can be restored. Aarav says it takes daily automated backups, stores them offsite, keeps at least 30 days of history and can roll a site back in under 10 minutes. That speed is Aarav's own claim, and I found nothing in the research that tests it.

Skynet's standard plans list an automated weekly backup, and Sagar Informatics lists a cloud backup without stating how often it runs. The studio's care page says backups are stored away from the server and that the restore process has actually been tested.

My reading: ask every provider two questions. When was the last restore done, and where are the copies kept? A backup on the same server as the site disappears in the same incident as the site.

## Monitoring: Uptime, Forms and Security Scans

Monitoring means a tool or a person checks the site on a schedule, so a failure is found by the provider and not by a customer. Aarav lists 24x7 uptime monitoring, a weekly end-to-end test of contact, quote, payment and signup paths, and malware scans every 24 hours.

Skynet's list includes checking that every enquiry form works and fixing broken links and crawling errors. The studio's care page states the purpose plainly: if the site goes down or the contact form stops delivering, the studio knows before customers do.

For an Indian small business, the forms and buttons that matter most are usually the ones that start a conversation. Test the enquiry form, the WhatsApp click-to-chat button, the call button, the Google Maps link and any UPI or card payment on a real phone. That list is my advice, not a provider's.

## How Long a Failure Lasts: A Worked Example

A failure lasts at most as long as the gap between checks, so the check interval decides how many enquiries are at risk. The inputs below are assumptions, not measurements: 3 contact-form enquiries a day, and a form that stops delivering right after a check. Replace the 3 with your own figure.

- **Form tested weekly** (Aarav's stated cadence): the longest it stays broken is 7 days, so 3 times 7 is 21 enquiries at risk.
- **Form tested monthly:** 30 days, so 3 times 30 is 90 enquiries at risk.
- **Never tested:** the gap lasts until you or a customer notices, and no limit applies.
- **Backups:** a daily backup loses at most 1 day of orders and form entries after a failure, and a weekly one up to 7 days.

At risk does not mean lost, because some visitors will phone or message instead. The arithmetic still shows why the interval, and not the word "monitoring", is the number to ask about.

## Content Changes: What Counts as Maintenance

Content changes are usually the part of maintenance you notice, because they cover edits to text, prices, photos and notices, in a quantity the plan caps. Sagar Informatics lists edits for promotions, price changes, new products and staff details, and Aarav promises same-day edits within a monthly cap.

Skynet gives a useful boundary. Its standard plans cover small changes, adding or removing images, updating pages and blog posts, and creating or updating forms. They do not cover dynamic functional development, major design changes, content writing, new marketing material or major migrations and version upgrades.

NTSPL argues that old information on a site can make visitors think the business has closed. That is the provider's opinion, but the practical point holds for a clinic or a restaurant: an old fee or timing on the page is a wrong answer given to every visitor.

## What Happens If You Skip Maintenance

Skipping maintenance lets each small fault run until someone outside the business finds it. The table sets what each skipped job exposes against the provider's own wording, and the last column is my reading of how it usually surfaces.

| Skipped job | What can fail | How you find out |
|---|---|---|
| Updates | Old code lets attackers in (Aarav) | A warning or a defaced page |
| Backups | No clean copy after a hack or bad update | The day you need one |
| Monitoring | Site down or form silent (Aarav, studio) | A customer calls |
| Links and indexing | Broken links and crawl errors (Skynet) | Traffic drops |
| Content | Old prices and timings (NTSPL, Sagar) | A customer quotes them |
| Speed | A heavy photo slows pages (studio) | A phone loads slowly |

Aarav also says every broken form is a lost lead and every broken link costs search visibility. Those are its own claims, written to sell a service. The mechanism is sound, but the research holds no independent measure of how often each fault happens.

## Real Failure Stories: What the Evidence Supports

No named, verifiable failure story appears in the five pages or in the studio's facts, so I will not present one. The studio's care page describes how sites decay: a plug-in goes out of date, a certificate expires, a form stops delivering, a page slows after someone uploads a 6 MB photo. It adds that none of these announce themselves.

**Documented:**
- Providers list these faults and say they are caught by monitoring and tests.
- Aarav says it runs a same-day clean-up if a breach is detected, which implies breaches happen.

**My reading, not a case:**
- Faults arrive one at a time, and each is small when it starts.
- The first signal is usually external: a customer, a search result or a warning.
- The cost grows with the gap between the fault and its discovery.

If you have lived through a failure, write down the date it began and the date it was found. That gap is the number the check intervals above are meant to shrink.

## Retainer, Pay-Per-Fix or Do It Yourself

Choose a retainer when the site takes enquiries, bookings or payments and nobody on your team can fix it. Skynet describes three models that frame the choice: do it in-house, outsource it, or a hybrid in which you edit content and the provider handles the technical work.

- **Do it yourself** when the site is a few static pages and you can log in to the hosting panel, renew the domain and restore a backup.
- **Pay per fix** when the site rarely changes and a day's delay costs little, accepting that no one is watching in between.
- **Retainer** when a day of silence costs more than a month of the plan, or when you want the checks to happen without remembering them.
- **Hybrid** when staff can change text and photos but not plugins, backups or security.

Whatever you choose, hold the logins yourself. The guide to [who owns your website](/blog/who-owns-my-website-code/) explains why the domain, hosting and admin accounts should be in your name.

## What to Do Next

Start with a health check of the site you have, then ask every provider the same questions in writing. Copy this list:

- [ ] Which updates run, how often, and are they tested on a staging copy first?
- [ ] How often are backups taken, where are they kept, and when was the last restore?
- [ ] What is monitored: uptime, forms, payments, the WhatsApp button?
- [ ] How many content edits are included, and what happens beyond them?
- [ ] What response time is promised for a site that is down?
- [ ] Is there a monthly report, and what does it show?
- [ ] Who holds the domain, hosting and admin logins?
- [ ] What does the contract exclude, and how do you cancel?

## FAQ

### What is website maintenance?

Website maintenance is the ongoing work of keeping a live site online, secure, current and working. It covers updates, backups, monitoring, security checks, speed work and content edits. AGTS describes it as the process of keeping a website up to date.

### What does website maintenance include?

It usually includes software updates, backups, uptime and form monitoring, security scans and a set number of content edits. Skynet Technologies also lists broken-link fixes, database optimisation and a monthly or quarterly report. Major redesigns and new features are normally excluded.

### How does website maintenance work?

A provider runs a repeating cycle of checks and updates, then reports what it did. AGTS describes review, security audit, updates, monitoring and support, and Aarav Infotech sends a monthly report.
### Does it cost money to maintain a website?

Yes, even a small site has recurring costs for its domain and hosting, and a site with plugins or payments needs updates and backups on top. Whether you pay a provider or spend your own time, the work still has to happen. The [cost guide](/blog/website-maintenance-charges/) sets out the figures.

### How much should I pay for website maintenance?

Pay for scope, not a flat number: which jobs are covered, how often backups run, how fast the provider responds and how many edits are included. The Aarav plans above show those four things changing from one plan to the next. The [cost guide](/blog/website-maintenance-charges/) has the sourced monthly ranges.

### How do I hire a website maintenance service?

Ask each provider the same written questions about updates, backups, monitoring, edits, response time, reports, logins and cancellation. Compare the answers, not the price alone. Ask for the last restore date and a client reference, because page claims cannot be verified.

### How do I maintain a website myself?

You can maintain a simple site yourself if you update its software, keep a tested backup off the server, renew the domain and check the forms. Set a calendar reminder for each job and its interval. If you skip any of those, you are in the position the failure table above describes.

### What happens if I never update my website?

Outdated software becomes the easiest way in for attackers, and Aarav Infotech says so directly. Broken links, old content and silent form failures also build up unnoticed. The research holds no independent figure for how often each happens.

### Can maintenance be done by someone who did not build the site?

Yes, after a health check of what is there. The studio says it maintains sites it did not build, and that it will tell you honestly what it finds and what would be needed first. You should still hold the logins yourself.

### Is a maintenance contract a lock-in?

It can be. Skynet says its contracts typically run six months or a year, so check the term and the cancellation rule before you sign. The studio says it has no retainer lock-ins unless you want one.
