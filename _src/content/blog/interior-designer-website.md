## What an Interior Designer Website Has to Do Before the First Call

An interior designer website has to answer a client's checking questions before they phone, because the money is committed in stages and the client knows it. Livspace, an Indian home interiors company, describes its own process on its site (read 16 November 2026). It runs from a free consultation or Experience Centre visit and a detailed costing, to a booking amount of 10 percent of the final quote (or a fixed minimum if higher), a cumulative 60 percent before execution starts, and the balance within 3 days of the dispatch notice.

Those are one large company's terms, not an industry rule. My reading is that a client facing a payment ladder like that wants three answers first: have you done work like mine, what happens after I enquire, and will my budget fit.

That high-ticket clients research before calling is the premise of this article, and no source I had measures it. March Branding, a design agency, makes the visual side of the point: a visitor who finds a cluttered site is unlikely to ask you to design their space.

## Project Case Pages: What Each One Should Contain

A project case page works when it answers four silent questions: what was the brief, what did you decide, what does it look like, and what happened next. March Branding reports that Studio Hollond gives every project its own page, and that Bernard London's Notebook page sets case studies beside design insights and press coverage. The sources do not say what those pages contain, so this template is my own.

1. **The brief.** Who the client was (a family, a clinic, a restaurant) and what they asked for, with their consent.
2. **The space.** Property type, area, city, and whether it was new or a renovation.
3. **The constraints.** Budget band, deadline, structural limits and anything that made it hard.
4. **The decisions.** Two or three choices you made and the reason for each.
5. **The photographs.** A wide shot, a detail shot and the plan, each large enough to judge.
6. **The timeline.** Start to handover in weeks, including delays you handled.
7. **The next step.** One button: enquire about a similar project.

If you cannot publish a budget, publish the scope: rooms, area and whether the work was new or a renovation. A page of pictures proves taste. A page with a brief, scope and timeline proves you can run a project.

Canva's template page advises showing completed projects with client testimonials, saying past work raises a prospective client's trust. That is a template seller's claim. Use a testimonial only with the client's permission and in their own words.

## Before and After Photos for Interior Projects

Before and after photos help on renovations, where the change is what the client paid for, and add little on a project that began as an empty shell. No source I read tests whether pairs raise enquiries, so I give no figure. These rules are my practice advice:

- **Same angle and height.** Shoot the after from the exact spot of the before.
- **Same light.** Compare morning with morning, or the pair misleads.
- **A caption that names the change.** Say what moved, what stayed and what it needed (a new slab, a relocated sink).
- **Written consent.** Occupied homes and businesses need the owner's agreement before any photograph is published.
- **Compressed files.** The studio's web development page notes most visitors arrive on a phone, often on patchy 4G, so heavy images cost you the visitor.

That last point pulls against the full-bleed imagery March praises on several sites. The fix is large images that are properly compressed, not small images.

The studio's Ashirwad Dental case study rests on the same principle: real photography of the clinic and its work, so nervous first-time patients know what to expect. The studio's article on [clinic websites in India](/blog/website-for-doctors-india/) covers consent and council rules for before and after photos; those rules bind clinics, not designers, but the habit of written consent transfers.

If you are a registered architect, check your professional council's advertising rules before publishing. I have no primary source on them, so I do not state what they say.

## Enquiry Qualification: The Form That Filters Before the Call

An enquiry form qualifies leads when it asks five to seven questions that decide whether a project fits your studio, so wrong-fit enquiries get a message and right-fit ones get a call. This is the gap in the ranking pages: the March review, the Canva page and Livspace's home page do not discuss it as a design choice.

Livspace shows the mechanism in its own tools. Its wardrobe calculator asks five questions: length, sliding or swing doors, finish, core material and smart accessories. Its home interior calculator takes dimensions, style and chosen features, then returns an itemised estimate. The studio's real estate industry page recommends forms that ask budget, timeline and configuration and send each lead to WhatsApp or email at once.

The table is my proposed form, built from those three patterns. Each question has a stated job.

| Question | Why you ask it | Answer type |
|---|---|---|
| Project type | Sets scope: new build, renovation or one room | Pick one |
| Area or rooms | Sizes the work | Number or range |
| City and locality | Decides site visits and travel | Short text |
| Budget band | Shows fit before any call | Ranges to pick |
| Handover month | Tests the timeline | Month |
| Who decides | Avoids price-shopping for a third party | Pick one |
| Contact channel | WhatsApp or phone, as the client prefers | Pick one |

Here is the arithmetic for what this can save, with assumed inputs to replace with your own log. Say 20 enquiries a month and a 20 minute first call: 20 times 20 is 400 minutes. If the answers show 6 enquiries are clearly out of range, 6 times 20 is 120 minutes saved, leaving 14 calls and 280 minutes.

Choose a published calculator when your work is modular and repeatable, as Livspace's kitchens and wardrobes are. Choose a budget band question when each job is bespoke. More fields usually mean fewer finished forms, though I have no sourced figure, so start with seven or fewer.

## Portfolio and Site Map for Interior Designers and Architects

An interior designer website portfolio needs about eight pages, and an architect's needs the same eight with a service page for each discipline. This is my site map, which fits what the studied sites do:

1. **Home.** One strong project, one sentence on who you serve, one action.
2. **Portfolio.** Projects filterable by type, with large images.
3. **Project pages.** One per project, built on the case template above.
4. **Services.** One page per discipline you sell.
5. **Process.** The steps after an enquiry, as Heanly Harris numbers them.
6. **People.** Who will actually work on the project.
7. **Journal.** Optional notes and press, as Bernard London does.
8. **Contact.** The qualifying form, WhatsApp and a map.

Studios in March's review show the options. Ben Pentreath's navigation names architecture, interiors and masterplanning, and Janine Stone offers architecture, luxury interior design, construction and project management. Taylor Howes runs separate brands for new homeowners and for homeware, linked from its homepage.

Keep one site with separate service pages when the same client buys several disciplines from you. Give a service its own brand when it sells to a different client, as Taylor Howes does.

## What Usually Goes Wrong on These Sites

Most interior design sites lose enquiries in the same few places, and each has a cheap fix. The causes come from the sources where they name one and from my reading elsewhere.

| Mistake | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Visitor sees no next step | An all-image front page, as March notes for one site | One visible action on every page |
| Photos too small to judge | A thumbnail grid | Large images, one project per page |
| Projects shown as pictures only | No brief, scope or timeline | Use the case template |
| Wrong client enquires | No signal on budget or scale | Tone and qualifying fields |
| Template left as supplied | The template's demo text and stock photos | Replace both with your own |
| Slow pages on a phone | Uncompressed full-bleed photos | Compress before upload |
| Claims you cannot back | Awards or press borrowed | Link every award and mention |

Martin Kemp's serious tone is the counterpoint: March says it suits his clients. The mistake is not seriousness or playfulness, it is a tone that does not match the client you want.

## What to Do Next

Turn one finished project into a case page first, then add the qualifying form and count for a month how many calls it spares you. Copy this checklist:

- [ ] One project has a page with brief, scope, timeline and large photographs.
- [ ] Each before and after pair shares angle and light, and the caption names the change.
- [ ] Every client photo and testimonial has written permission.
- [ ] The form asks seven questions or fewer, including a budget band.
- [ ] Answers reach WhatsApp and email together.
- [ ] Every page shows one clear next action.
- [ ] I checked my council's advertising rules if I am a registered architect.

### How do I create an interior design website?

Start with one finished project as a case page, then add a portfolio, services, process and contact page with a qualifying form. Choose a template if you have few projects and can write and photograph yourself, and a custom build if you want the form routed and tracked.

### What is the best interior designer website?

There is no single best site, because each solves a different problem. March Branding's review of 25 sites is a starting list, and the table above names what eight of them do well. Judge any site by whether a visitor finds a project, the process and a next step.

### Can I make an interior designer website for free?

Yes, with a template: Canva says its interior design templates are free to customise and publish, and Figma's community lists free ones. Check the template's forms, WhatsApp and Maps options first, because Canva's page does not mention them. A free site still costs your time to write, photograph and maintain.

### What pages does an interior designer website need?

Eight pages cover most studios: home, portfolio, project pages, services, process, people, an optional journal and contact. Architects add a service page for each discipline. This is my site map, not a sourced rule.

### Should an interior designer show before and after photos?

Show them on renovations, where the change is what the client paid for, and skip them where the project began as an empty shell. Shoot both from the same angle in the same light and caption what changed. Get written consent from the owner first.

### What should an interior designer enquiry form ask?

Ask project type, area or rooms, city, budget band, handover month, who decides and the preferred contact channel. These seven questions let you reply to wrong-fit enquiries by message. Keep the form short, since more fields usually mean fewer completed forms.
