Fixed price vs hourly development comes down to one question: who pays when the work takes longer than planned. On a fixed-price contract you agree the scope and the total before work starts, and the developer absorbs overruns. On hourly billing you pay for the hours worked and carry that risk yourself. The qualification that matters is that a fixed price is only fixed for the written scope, so the contract wording counts for more than the label.

- **Fixed price.** Best when you can list every page and feature. Changes outside the scope are priced separately, and the quote usually carries a buffer for risk (Hunchbite puts it at 20 to 40 percent).
- **Hourly.** Best when requirements will change. It needs a budget cap and weekly time reports to stay safe.
- **Retainer.** Best for ongoing work, because you reserve a set number of hours each month.
- **Price.** Elyx quotes fixed-price builds to your requirements, with engagements starting from {{start}}. The [pricing page](/pricing/) explains how, and a fixed quote is confirmed in writing after a call.
- **Evidence.** Every source below is a vendor or practitioner page, not an independent study, so the figures are claims to test.

## How Fixed Price, Hourly and Retainer Contracts Work

The three models differ in what the money buys: a fixed price buys a defined outcome, hourly buys time, and a retainer buys reserved availability. The table sets them side by side using the definitions on the pages read for this article on 17 November 2026.

| Model | What you pay for | Who carries overrun risk | Other names |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fixed price | An agreed scope for one total | The developer | Flat fee, package, block |
| Hourly | Hours worked times a rate | You | Time and materials |
| Retainer | Committed hours each week or month | You, within agreed hours | Dedicated team, monthly plan |

Designz Lab calls hourly and time and materials the same thing, and calls a dedicated team a monthly fee for a set amount of a team's time. Arc.dev describes a retainer as an hourly rate plus a commitment to a stated number of hours and weeks.

Search suggestions for this topic also throw up "firm fixed price" and "labor hour". These are formal procurement terms, and none of the eight pages read here defines them. My reading is that they map loosely onto fixed price and hourly, but in a private web or app contract, ask the vendor to define any such term in writing.

## Fixed Price: Certainty With a Buffer Built In

A fixed-price contract gives you a known total for a written scope, and the developer pays for any overrun inside that scope. Hunchbite lists the usual steps: discovery, a written specification of what is in and out, one quote covering design, development, testing and deployment, a change process, and milestone payments.

The certainty has a cost. Hunchbite says teams build a 20 to 40 percent buffer into fixed quotes, so you pay for risk cover whether or not it is used. Designz Lab agrees the buffer can make a fixed price look higher than an optimistic hourly estimate, but argues that hourly projects often overrun, so the fixed total is frequently lower. Neither page publishes data for that claim.

The second risk is quality. Kambo Associates names corners cut to protect the price as the main risk of fixed price, and advises checking the work at each milestone. Hunchbite adds that a tight quote can leave technical debt for you to inherit.

A practical defence is to ask for the quote broken down by phase. Hunchbite suggests this because it shows where the buffer sits without undoing the fixed-price model.

## Hourly Billing: Flexibility That Needs a Cap

Hourly billing means you pay the hours worked at an agreed rate, so you keep the freedom to change direction and carry the risk of the bill growing. Kambo Associates says it suits projects whose requirements will change, such as new web applications, provided you set a budget cap and ask for weekly time reports.

Four controls make hourly safe, all from Kambo Associates and Hunchbite:

1. **A cap.** Hunchbite's wording: bill hourly, alert at 80 percent of budget and stop at 100 percent unless both sides agree to continue.
2. **Task-level reports.** Weekly reports that list tasks, not only totals.
3. **A written rate and billing unit.** Kambo Associates suggests agreeing the minimum unit, for example 15 or 30 minutes.
4. **Frequent demos.** Review working software every week or two, so hours are tied to visible progress.

Do not compare hourly quotes by rate alone. iApp Technologies shows that 1,500 hours at $75 is $112,500, and so is 900 hours at $125 (75 × 1,500 and 125 × 900). The dearer rate can be the cheaper team if it needs fewer hours.

If the developer is abroad, Arc.dev notes that time-zone overlap, worker classification and tax withholding complicate hourly work. Confirm the tax side with an accountant before you pay. Zistemo, which writes about timesheets and invoicing, also offers a daily rate as a variant that avoids minute-level timesheets.

## Retainers: When a Monthly Fee Beats Both

A retainer works best for ongoing work where the tasks vary but you want guaranteed availability each month. Arc.dev's example is a developer committed to 20 hours a week for 10 weeks, with terms for overflow: a higher rate for extra hours, or unused hours rolling into the next week.

Arc.dev argues a retainer is usually the strongest option for long-term work, but it also sells a freelance platform, so weigh that. Hourly alone, Arc.dev notes, guarantees no hours, so the developer owes you no priority over other clients.

Designz Lab places the dedicated-team retainer with long-running products that need continuous development. Many products, it says, start fixed-price for version one and move to a monthly retainer once live.

My reading: a retainer is a poor fit for a first build with a deadline, and a good fit for maintenance, updates and steady feature work after launch.

## Fixed Price vs Hourly Development When the Scope Changes

When scope changes, an hourly bill rises with the hours, while a fixed price stays put until the change falls outside the written scope. The table uses iApp Technologies' example of 500 hours at $100 an hour, which gives $50,000 (500 × 100). The third row is my arithmetic.

| Situation | Hourly (USD) | Fixed (USD) |
|---|---|---|
| Work takes the 500 hours planned | 50,000 | 50,000 |
| Work inside scope takes 600 hours | 60,000 | 50,000 |
| You add 100 hours of new features | 60,000 | 50,000 plus a priced change |

The second row is the real difference. A login screen that turns out harder than expected is your cost under hourly and the developer's under fixed. Adding scope costs you more under both, because Kambo Associates and Designz Lab both say changes outside the agreed scope are quoted separately.

The buffer cuts the other way. At Hunchbite's 20 to 40 percent, a fixed quote for the same work would be $60,000 to $70,000 (50,000 × 1.2 and 50,000 × 1.4). Fixed price only wins on cost if hourly overruns by more than the buffer.

How often does that happen? Chop Dawg says about 70 percent of software projects exceed budget by an average of 27 percent, without naming the studies, and iApp Technologies cites a 2024 BCG study in which nearly half of respondents saw more than 30 percent of projects delayed or over budget. A 27 percent average sits inside the buffer range, so on average the two models may cost about the same, though that is my reading, not a finding.

Hourly is stronger when the work shrinks. Chop Dawg notes a feature that turns out simpler, or is dropped, simply costs less by the hour, while vchuy-develop says a fixed price stays the same even if the scope turns out smaller.

## Which Model Fits Your Project

Choose fixed price when you can write the scope down, hourly when you are still discovering it, and a retainer when the work is ongoing. The table adapts Kambo Associates' scenarios, with the rows from other sources marked.

| Situation | Better fit | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Ten-page site, content known | Fixed price | Scope is easy to write down (Kambo Associates) |
| Standard online store | Fixed price | Known features, known effort (Kambo Associates) |
| New booking platform, unclear features | Fixed first phase, then hourly | Needs change as users test (Kambo Associates) |
| Unknown bug on an old site | Hourly with a cap | Effort unknown until investigated (Kambo Associates) |
| Monthly updates and small fixes | Retainer or monthly plan | Work is ongoing and varies (Kambo Associates) |
| Research-heavy or unproven idea | Hourly | Forced decisions too early otherwise (Hunchbite) |

Hunchbite's decision path reduces to three questions asked in order, and the first yes decides the model:

1. **Is the scope clear?** Yes means fixed price.
2. **Can one or two weeks of discovery make it clear?** Yes means paid discovery, then fixed price.
3. **Does someone on your side have the skill to judge hours?** Yes means capped hourly, and no means phased fixed price.

Hybrids are common, and each source offers one:

- **Fixed price with priced changes.** Each change becomes its own fixed addition (Hunchbite).
- **Capped hourly.** An estimate of 200 hours, billed at actuals, with a ceiling of 220 hours without written approval (Hunchbite).
- **Block then hourly.** Fix the audit and key templates, then bill the unknown parts hourly (vchuy-develop).
- **Phased fixed price.** Two-week phases, each with its own price and deliverable. Hunchbite calls this its recommended default, which is also what it sells.

## What Usually Goes Wrong, and How to Fix It

Contract trouble usually starts with unclear scope, not with the pricing model. Hunchbite makes the point directly: a vague fixed-price scope leads to disputes, and a vague hourly scope leads to waste.

| What goes wrong | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Argument over what is included | No written scope (Designz Lab) | List pages, features, integrations and exclusions |
| Cheap quote that grows | Components left out (iApp Technologies) | Compare scope line by line, not totals |
| Corners cut to hold the price | Fixed price squeezed (Kambo Associates) | Check quality at each milestone |
| Hours rising, progress flat | No cap (Hunchbite) | Set 80 and 100 percent alerts |
| Rework from late content | Content arrives after build (Kambo Associates) | Agree who supplies content and by when |
| Whole amount paid upfront | No payment link to progress (Designz Lab) | Tie payments to visible work |

Designz Lab also flags a quote with no revision rounds, no mention of testing or deployment, and no statement about who owns the code. Each is cheap to fix before signing and expensive afterwards.

## A Pre-Signing Checklist for Any Development Contract

Get these answers in writing before you sign, whichever model the developer proposes. The list draws on Designz Lab and Hunchbite, plus two Indian additions.

- [ ] What is included: design, development, testing, deployment, documentation and post-launch support.
- [ ] What is excluded: hosting, third-party services, content and ongoing maintenance.
- [ ] How many revision rounds are included, and how a change is priced.
- [ ] What triggers each payment, and whether the final one waits for your acceptance.
- [ ] Who pays if the work exceeds the estimate, and whether there is a cap.
- [ ] What happens if a deadline is missed.
- [ ] Who owns the code once you have paid.
- [ ] For hourly: the rate, the minimum billing unit and a weekly task-level report.
- [ ] Whether the quote is inclusive or exclusive of 18 percent GST, so you compare like with like.
- [ ] For a developer abroad: the currency and any tax withholding, confirmed with an accountant.

Write a one-page scope first, then ask for quotes under the model that fits it. List must-have features for the first release separately from later ones, which iApp Technologies recommends for keeping either model under control.

## FAQ

### What is the difference between fixed-price and hourly software development?

Fixed price sets the scope and total before work starts, so the developer carries overrun risk, while hourly bills the hours worked, so you carry it. Designz Lab and Hunchbite both frame the trade as budget certainty against scope flexibility.

### Is fixed-price software development more expensive?

A fixed price often looks higher at the start because the quote includes a risk buffer, which Hunchbite puts at 20 to 40 percent. Whether it ends up dearer depends on how far an hourly project would have overrun, and no source supplied here measures that.

### Is fixed price or hourly better for a website?

Fixed price is usually better for a business website or standard store, because the pages and features can be written down in advance. Kambo Associates recommends hourly for projects whose requirements will change, such as new web applications, with a cap in place.

### How are fixed-price software projects paid?

Fixed-price projects are usually paid in milestones. Designz Lab describes 30 to 50 percent to start, one or two payments on delivered milestones, and the last payment at launch after you accept the work.

### What happens if the scope changes on a fixed-price project?

A change inside the agreed scope is covered, and a change outside it is quoted separately. Designz Lab gives a new user role, screen, integration or report as typical changes, and a button colour or reordered form fields as typical inclusions.

### How do I control costs on an hourly project?

Set a budget cap, ask for weekly reports that list tasks, review working software every week or two, and agree the rate and minimum billing unit in writing. Hunchbite's version is an alert at 80 percent of budget and a stop at 100 percent.

### Is a retainer or hourly better if I want a developer long-term?

A retainer is usually better, because it reserves a set number of hours each month, while hourly guarantees none. Arc.dev makes that case but also runs a freelance platform, so weigh the source.

### What does time and materials mean?

Time and materials is another name for hourly pricing: you pay the hours worked at an agreed rate, plus costs such as licences or paid services. Kambo Associates notes it moves overrun risk from the developer to you.

### Can I combine fixed price and hourly?

Yes, and several sources recommend it. A common pattern is a fixed-price first version with core features, then hourly or monthly work based on real user feedback, or paid discovery followed by a fixed price.
