How to Validate a SaaS Idea Before Writing a Line of Code

How to validate a SaaS idea with interviews, a landing-page test, a waitlist and a paid pre-sale, with pass marks and a worked example in numbers.

To validate a SaaS idea before you build it, collect evidence of what people do, not what they say: hold about ten problem interviews, put up a landing page that asks for a signup, follow every signup with one question, and ask the interested ones to pay a deposit or sign a paid pilot. The honest qualification is that no test proves success, and most figures below come from tool vendors and founders' own accounts, not audited studies. My reading is that the order of the tests matters more than any single number.

  • Four tests. Interviews, a landing page, a waitlist follow-up and a pre-sale, each stronger than the last.
  • Behaviour beats opinion. A signup is a weak signal, a reply is better, and money is the strongest.
  • Pass marks. Sourced marks are attributed; the rest are my own, and labelled.
  • Cost. Most of it is days of your time, not rupees.

What "Validating a SaaS Idea" Means, and What It Cannot Prove

Validating a SaaS idea means showing, with evidence from real people, that a specific customer has a problem painful enough to pay to fix. Preetam Nath, who writes a free micro-SaaS guide, says talking to users is not enough and that real-world behaviour, such as hours spent on the problem every week, is the only foolproof check.

Supastarter's idea validator says on its own page that its score is a heuristic, not proof of demand. Preuve, which sells validation reports, says only 5.8% of 846 scans scored 75 or more and blames the product angle, the customer definition and the sales channel, not the product itself (its own July 2026 benchmark; I could not see its method).

Validated SaaS Ideas Start With One Written Assumption

Validated SaaS ideas start as one sentence naming a customer, a problem and a reason to pay, written before any test. Indie Hackers asks for the same three answers: who exactly the audience is, what the core pain is and why they would pay.

Use this template, with a made-up example after it:

  • Template. For [customer], [problem] costs [time or money] every [week], they handle it today with [workaround], and they would pay [amount] a month to stop.
  • Fictional example. For physiotherapy clinics with two to five therapists, missed appointments cost a few sessions a week, front desks chase them on WhatsApp, and the owner would pay a monthly fee for automatic reminders.

Preuve warns that "small business" is too vague: a five-person agency and a hundred-person manufacturer share almost nothing. The studio's SaaS page lists turning spreadsheet and WhatsApp-group workflows into software as part of what it builds, which is a good place to look for a workflow you can observe.

Customer Interviews: Who to Ask and What to Ask

A validation interview is a conversation about how the person handles the problem today, and you do not pitch in it. Indie Hackers says to talk to at least 10 potential users, and Eddie Larsen on Medium says 5 to 10 real people, not your mother.

Preetam Nath's rule is the useful one: never ask whether someone needs your idea, ask about the pain, the context and the current fix. He recommends The Mom Test by Rob Fitzpatrick for this. Ask these, in order:

  1. Walk me through the last time you had to do this task.
  2. What do you use today, and what does it cost you in time or money?
  3. What is the most annoying part?
  4. Have you tried to fix it? What happened?
  5. How often does it come up?
  6. Who else decides what gets bought?
  7. Would you pay to solve it? (Indie Hackers marks this one as important; ask it last.)

Preetam Nath lists where to find people: subreddits, Facebook groups, Slack groups, LinkedIn and niche forums. My reading is that in India much of this talk sits in WhatsApp groups and trade associations, so ask a member to introduce you, then offer a call or a voice note.

For B2B products, IdeaProof says interview the end user and the budget owner separately. Its figures are 5 to 10 percent response to cold outreach, so about 200 qualified decision makers for 15 interviews, which is a vendor's estimate with no method shown.

Reading Interview Answers: Green Lights and Red Flags

An answer is a green light when it describes a specific recent episode and a real cost, and a red flag when it is polite. The table is my reading of the signals Eddie Larsen and Preetam Nath describe, plus the usual traps.

What you hearReadingBasis
"Sounds cool!"A polite brush-offEddie Larsen
"I might use it someday"A noEddie Larsen

Why is five yeses not enough? Because opinions cost the speaker nothing, and Preetam Nath's Henry Ford line makes the same point: ask about behaviour, not wants.

The Landing-Page Test: What to Put on the Page

A landing-page test shows whether strangers respond to your promise, which interviews cannot show because the people you interviewed already agreed to talk. The page needs a headline naming the customer and the pain, three outcomes, a price or price range (my reading: a price filters out the curious) and one action.

SaaStock suggests A/B testing headlines, calls to action and value propositions and measuring conversion. Indie Hackers names Carrd, Wix or Notion for the page and Mailchimp or ConvertKit for emails, and one founder reports 47 signups and 3 willing beta testers in two days, a self-reported result.

For traffic, Preetam Nath suggests Keywords Everywhere (about $10 of credits), Google Ads, Facebook interest ads and subreddit search. He warns that search volume mixes casual and serious searchers: "online video editor" is mostly consumers wanting a free tool.

IdeaProof says a B2B test costs $1,500 to $3,000 in ads and should reach a 5% click-through rate and a 15% waitlist conversion; falling short suggests a weak message or a crowded field. Treat those as a vendor's rule of thumb.

Waitlists: What a Signup Proves and What It Does Not

A waitlist signup proves a stranger gave you an email address, which is the weakest piece of evidence in this article. Preetam Nath counts a filled form as validation, and SaaStock says to watch sign-up rates and email engagement; I treat both as the first rung only.

The ladder below ranks what a person gives you. The ranking is mine, built on Preetam Nath's behaviour argument.

RungWhat the person givesStrength
Joins the waitlistAn email, secondsWeak
Answers one questionA few minutes of thoughtMedium
Books a callAn hour of timeMedium to strong
Pays a deposit or a pilotMoneyStrong

Eddie Larsen warns that lukewarm people call an idea "interesting" and then vanish. So email every signup within a day with one question, "what are you using today?", and a link to book a call.

Signing up for a product people know little about is a real hurdle, and it is a question founders raise in the Reddit threads behind this research. Give them a reason: early access, or a free one-to-one setup.

Pre-Sales: Getting Paid Before the Product Exists

A pre-sale asks someone to pay, or sign a paid pilot, before the product exists, and it is the strongest test because the customer risks something. SaaStock describes pre-sales as discounts or exclusive access for early commitment, with a lifetime discount for early adopters as its example.

For B2B, IdeaProof recommends a letter of intent or a paid pilot of $1,000 to $5,000 for a 30-day proof of concept, and calls three paid pilots in 60 days strong evidence. My reading: three is a small sample and the sums are enterprise-sized, so scale the amount to your buyer.

Choose a deposit when the buyer is a person or a small firm, and a paid pilot or letter of intent when a company has procurement steps. In India a UPI payment link is the natural way to take a deposit.

Write down what they get, by when, and the refund if you do not deliver. Ask a chartered accountant how an advance is invoiced and whether GST applies, because this article gives no tax advice. Never imply the product is live when it is not.

The conversion rates are 3 / 8 = 37.5% of calls, 3 / 45 = 6.7% of signups and 3 / 300 = 1.0% of visitors. If nobody paid, the same 45 signups would still say "interest without commitment", and the next move is more interviews, not code.

Build, Retest or Stop: Decision Rules

Build only when strangers sign up, answer, talk and pay, and retest or stop when any step breaks. The table maps results to readings and next moves; it is a framework, not a sourced benchmark.

ResultReadingNext move
Few signups from targeted trafficWeak message or wrong audienceRewrite the headline, change the channel
Many signups, few repliesCuriosity, not painGo back to interviews

The last row follows Preetam Nath, who says that if you cannot find where users gather, you may find out fast that they do not exist. He also says an idea needed by only 10 or 100 people cannot become a business, and looks for thousands of users and a few hundred customers.

What to Do Next

Spend the next week on the four tests and decide on a set date. This schedule is my suggestion.

  1. Day 1. Write the one-sentence assumption.
  2. Days 1 to 5. Hold about ten interviews and note the answers.
  3. Days 3 to 4. Publish the landing page and send it to the people you interviewed and their circles.
  4. Days 5 to 6. Email every signup one question and a call link.
  5. Days 6 to 7. Offer the pre-sale to everyone who booked a call.

Copy this checklist:

  • [ ] One sentence names customer, problem, workaround and price.
  • [ ] I held about ten interviews and logged red flags.
  • [ ] The page names the pain and has one action.
  • [ ] Every signup got one question within a day.
  • [ ] I asked for money, with a refund line.
  • [ ] I wrote my pass marks before looking at results.

Frequently asked questions

How do I validate a SaaS idea?

Validate a SaaS idea by running four tests in order: problem interviews, a landing-page signup test, a one-question follow-up to signups, and a paid pre-sale. Each test is stronger evidence than the one before. Stop as soon as a test fails.

How can I validate a SaaS idea without spending money?

You can validate a SaaS idea for almost nothing by interviewing people and using free page and email tools. Indie Hackers names Carrd, Wix or Notion for the page, and Eddie Larsen's method is a gut check, 5 to 10 conversations and a simple landing page.

How do I validate a SaaS idea on Reddit?

Search the relevant subreddit for people describing the problem, then message them to ask for a one-to-one conversation. Preetam Nath describes this approach and says you need not post at all. Lead with their problem, not your product.

How many people should I talk to before building?

Talk to about 10. Indie Hackers says at least 10 potential users, Eddie Larsen says 5 to 10, and IdeaProof's cold B2B route aims for 15 interviews. Stop only when the answers start to repeat.

Is it enough if five people say it is a great idea?

No, because praise costs the speaker nothing. Eddie Larsen lists "sounds cool" and "I might use it someday" as red flags, and Preetam Nath says only behaviour validates an idea. Look for hours spent, money spent or a deposit.

How do I find a SaaS idea worth validating?

Watch how people in a trade you know already struggle, then look for a workflow done by hand. The studio's SaaS page lists turning spreadsheet and WhatsApp-group workflows into software as part of its work. Your first job is checking that the pain repeats.

How do I validate a SaaS idea when nobody will give feedback?

Offer something in return and ask smaller. Indie Hackers says people open up when you give them personal time, and Preetam Nath says that if you cannot find any place where your users gather, the market may not exist.

Can an AI idea validator validate my SaaS idea?

An AI validator can sharpen your assumptions but cannot prove demand. Supastarter says its score is a heuristic and only customer behaviour proves demand, and IdeaProof says its score is decision support, not a probability of success.
