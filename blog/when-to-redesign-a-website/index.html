9 Signs Your Business Website Needs a Redesign

When to redesign a website: nine signs with a test for each, a refresh, revamp or rebuild table, and steps that help protect your Google rankings.

When to redesign a website comes down to one test: a problem you can measure keeps costing you enquiries, and a smaller fix has not removed it. The nine signs below are slow pages, a broken phone layout, visitors who do not enquire, a dated look, a site you cannot edit, old software, a changed business, falling search traffic and complaints. The honest qualification is that the five pages I read are written by firms that sell design, builders or agency services, and none is Indian, so the counting rule here is my reading, not a sourced benchmark.

  • Nine signs. Each has a test you can run on your own phone or in free tools.
  • Looks are the weakest sign. One designer in our sources says almost none of the deciding signals are about how the site looks.
  • Match the fix to the finding. A dated look is a refresh, a weak journey is a revamp, and a platform nobody can edit is a rebuild.
  • Protect your rankings. Map every old address to a new one and redirect it before launch.

When to Redesign a Website: The Nine-Sign Check

Nine signs say it is time to redesign, and each can be tested today without paying anyone. The table gives a test for each and how many of the five pages named it, as read on 17 November 2026.

SignTest you can runPages naming it
1. Slow on a phoneOpen it on mobile data and time the headline4 of 5
2. Broken on mobileTap every button on a real phone4 of 5
3. No enquiriesDivide enquiries by real visitors4 of 5
4. Looks datedCompare it with your brand and signboard today4 of 5
5. You cannot edit itChange a price and a phone number yourself3 of 5

My counting rule is not a sourced threshold: score one point for each sign that is true today. Butterfly, an Australian agency, says that when three or more of its eight signs apply, small updates are unlikely to be enough. Zero to two points means fix the leak you can name; three or more means ask for a written audit before any design work.

Signs 1 and 2: Slow or Broken on a Phone

A page that is slow or awkward on a phone loses the visitor before the offer is read. The studio's web development page says most visitors arrive on a phone, often on a patchy 4G connection, and that one 6 MB photo can slow a page.

Freshpies, a UK agency, says 40% of users abandon a page that takes more than three seconds to load, citing unnamed research, so treat it as direction, not a threshold. Butterfly adds that failing Core Web Vitals, Google's measures of how fast a page loads and responds, hurts both visitors and rankings.

Run this test on a real phone:

  1. Switch off Wi-Fi, open your homepage and count the seconds until the headline and the contact button both show.
  2. Run the page through Google PageSpeed Insights. Elementor says a mobile score far below the desktop score points to a site not built mobile-first.
  3. Read a page without zooming, then tap every button with a thumb.

Lauren Lester, a web designer, says slow pages should first be fixed through plug-ins and settings, and a missing mobile layout is usually a platform fix. Heavy images are a clean-up; a layout built only for desktop is a rebuild.

Sign 3: Visitors Arrive but Do Not Enquire

A site needs a redesign for conversion when real visitors arrive and almost none call, message on WhatsApp or fill the form. A conversion rate is the share of visitors who take the action you want.

Butterfly's example sets a site converting at 1% against a sector benchmark of 3% and calls the gap calculable lost revenue. Butterfly gives no source for the 3%, so I use it only as an illustration. The table shows the arithmetic with invented inputs; replace them with your own counts.

StepArithmeticResult
Real visitors a monthFrom your analytics1,000
Enquiries at 1%1,000 x 0.0110
Enquiries at 3%1,000 x 0.0330
Monthly gap30 minus 1020 enquiries
Customers, if 1 in 4 buys20 x 0.255 customers

Multiply the 5 customers by your average sale to price the gap. Count only real visitors, as the article on why a cheap website is not getting customers explains.

Elementor names the pattern: decent traffic, about 10 seconds or less on a page, and no leads. Freshpies says the cause may be layout, navigation or calls to action, so try moving the main action to the first screen before a rebuild. Figma advises recording form completion rates before you redesign, so you can tell afterwards whether it worked.

Sign 4: The Site Looks Outdated

A dated look is a real sign, but on its own it usually calls for a refresh, not a rebuild. Freshpies lists heavy gradients, flashing text and busy layouts as marks of an old design, and Elementor flags a site untouched for more than two years.

Lauren Lester notes that owners who say "I hate my website" usually mean the look, and assume that means starting over. Butterfly's test for a refresh asks three things: is the content accurate, is the structure logical and do the key journeys work? If all three are yes, a visual update may be enough.

In local trades the look carries trust. The studio's clinic page says real photos of the clinic and team turn a nervous visitor into a booking.

Sign 5: You Cannot Edit It Yourself

If you must call a developer to change a price, a phone number or a batch date, the site is costing you time and you should act. Freshpies lists a site that needs a developer for every small change as a redesign sign, and Elementor adds plug-in and theme updates that break the site.

Lauren Lester separates two causes. If the barrier is training or a poor theme, a refresh with training fixes it. If the site is custom-coded and nobody on your team can touch it without breaking something, she calls it a rebuild.

Test it yourself:

  • Time it. Change a phone number, a price and one photo without help, and note the minutes.
  • Count last year. List the changes you needed that the site made hard or impossible.

The studio names "nobody on the owner's side can update them" as one of three common failures in small-business sites. Elyx Solutions' web development service adds a content management system where it makes sense and records a short walkthrough.

Signs 6 to 9: Old Software, a Changed Business, Falling Search Traffic and Complaints

Four more signs are less visible than speed or looks, and each has a plain test.

  • 6. Old software and bugs. Elementor lists an unsupported plug-in, redirect loops, frequent downtime and updates that break the site, and Figma adds security risk from outdated code. For repeated hacks, Lauren Lester says update plug-ins and improve hosting first, and rebuild only if custom code has nobody to maintain it. Test: submit your own form and check the email arrives.
  • 7. The business has changed. Elementor describes enquiries from people you cannot serve, or one offer taking all the attention. Test: read the homepage aloud against what you sell this year.
  • 8. Search traffic is falling. Butterfly says declining organic traffic can come from crawl errors, broken redirects and poor structure. My reading: check whether the fall started after a site change, because a redesign cannot fix a drop with another cause.
  • 9. Customers or staff complain. Elementor lists complaints from visitors and customers as a sign. Test: tally complaints and "the website says..." corrections for one month.

Butterfly also lists a missing accessibility standard, but its example is the Australian public sector, so I left it out of the nine. Figma's baseline is still worth asking for: keyboard-friendly navigation, alt text and enough colour contrast.

Signs That Are Not Enough on Their Own

Three common reasons are weak alone: the site's age, a competitor's newer look and your own taste. Lauren Lester says anyone who answers "every three years" is guessing, and that a well-built site on a modern platform has no natural expiry.

Butterfly argues the opposite emphasis: an underperforming site is not neutral, and each month of delay adds technical debt. They differ partly because Butterfly sells large redesigns and Lauren Lester sells audits and refreshes. If your site passes all nine tests and enquiries are steady, leave it alone and keep it maintained.

Refresh, Revamp or Rebuild: Which Fix Fits Each Sign

Match the fix to the finding: a dated look alone is a refresh, a weak journey on a sound site is a revamp, and a platform nobody can change is a rebuild. Butterfly defines a refresh as cosmetic, a revamp as better UX, structure and performance on the same platform, and a redesign as rethinking goals, audience, technology and design from the ground up.

The sources disagree on the word itself: Lauren Lester uses "redesign" for a visual update and "rebuild" for starting over. Ask any vendor which of the three they are quoting.

Redesign a Website Without Losing SEO: Seven Steps

A redesign keeps its Google rankings when every old address is mapped to a new one and redirected before launch. Butterfly calls launching without 301 redirects, which are permanent redirects that send visitors and Google from the old address to the new one, the most damaging and most common mistake.

Butterfly says Google's own site-move documentation asks for URL mapping and 301 redirects before launch; I have not read that page myself. Lauren Lester says a refresh that keeps URLs, page titles and content carries minimal SEO risk. The studio says it keeps what earns traffic, including URLs and content, and rebuilds the rest.

  1. Export every live address from your sitemap and Search Console.
  2. Save three months of clicks and enquiries as a benchmark, as Butterfly advises.
  3. Map each old address to the page that replaces it.
  4. Carry over titles, meta descriptions, headings and structured data.
  5. Set up the 301 redirects before launch, not after.
  6. Match the address on your Google Business Profile to the new site (my reading).
  7. Watch results for 30 to 90 days, as Butterfly suggests.

Butterfly adds that even a well-run redesign can cause temporary ranking swings, so the aim is to shorten them, not promise zero change.

Redesign Services and Agencies: Questions to Ask Before You Pay

Ask every agency or freelancer the same five questions and compare the written answers. The article on hiring a web developer in India goes further.

  • Which fix? Is the quote a refresh, a revamp or a rebuild?
  • Which addresses stay? Ask for the URL map and redirect plan.
  • Who edits what? Ask for an editing screen and a walkthrough.
  • What follows launch? The studio's Launch tier includes 30 days of support.
  • What is the total? A fixed written quote, with GST stated.

What to Do Next

Spend one week testing before you spend anything on design. Work through the steps in order:

  1. Day 1. Run the phone speed and tap tests (signs 1 and 2).
  2. Day 2. Set up Search Console and GA4, then list 12 months of clicks and enquiries (signs 3 and 8).
  3. Day 3. Time your own edits and submit your own form (signs 5 and 6).
  4. Day 4. Read the homepage against what you sell now and tally complaints (signs 4, 7 and 9).
  5. Day 5. Score the nine signs and find your row in the fix table.
  6. Days 6 and 7. If you scored three or more, brief a builder with your findings and ask the five questions.

If the audit says redesign, the studio's web development service covers redesigns, and its Ashirwad Dental case study shows one: 8 weeks from kickoff to launch, with a WhatsApp booking flow and a gallery. That is one case, not a promise of a result.

Frequently asked questions

How do I know if my website needs a redesign?

Your website needs a redesign when several of the nine signs are true and a smaller fix has not worked. Score one point per sign: three or more means get a written audit, and zero to two means fix the named leak first.

How often should a website be redesigned?

There is no fixed interval. Lauren Lester says "every three years" is a guess, while Elementor flags a site untouched for more than two years. Judge it by the nine tests, not the calendar.

Should I redesign my website?

Redesign it if the tests show a problem a refresh or revamp cannot clear, such as a platform nobody can edit. If it loads fast, works on a phone, brings steady enquiries and you can edit it, leave it and keep it maintained.

How much does a website redesign cost?

Elyx engagements start from ₹9,999, excluding GST. A business website of 5 to 10 pages or a landing page is quoted to your requirements, and a fixed quote follows a call.

How long does a website redesign take?

The studio's draft timeline is 2–4 weeks for a business website and 1–2 weeks for a landing page, agreed in writing first. Its Ashirwad Dental redesign took 8 weeks from kickoff to launch, and Butterfly's mid-complexity redesigns run 14 to 24 weeks.

What is the difference between a refresh, a revamp and a redesign?

A refresh updates looks, a revamp improves UX, structure and performance on the same platform, and a redesign rethinks everything, per Butterfly. Lauren Lester uses "redesign" for a visual update, so always ask what a quote covers.

How do I redesign a website without losing SEO?

Map every old address to its new page, set 301 redirects before launch, carry over titles and headings, and record a benchmark first. Butterfly says missing redirects are the most damaging mistake and that temporary ranking swings can still happen.

How do I plan a website redesign?

Plan it in five phases: discovery and research, planning and strategy, design, development and testing, then launch and monitoring. That is Figma's sequence. Start with the nine tests and agree the numbers that will show success.

Can AI redesign my website for me?

AI tools can draft a layout, but my research holds no test of how well they redesign a real business site. Lauren Lester's note on AI-built sites asks who checks the forms, SEO and accessibility, and that still needs a person.
