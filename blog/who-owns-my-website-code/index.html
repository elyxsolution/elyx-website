Who Owns Your Website? Domain, Code and Content Ownership Explained

Who owns my website code, domain and content? A sourced India checklist of ownership traps, lock-in signs and the handover list to ask for.

Who owns my website code? Whoever your written agreement and the account names say owns it, which is not always the person who paid. Paying a developer does not by itself move the domain, hosting, code or logins into your name, and in India an outside developer may keep the rights in the code they wrote unless they assign them to you in writing. The honest qualification is that this page rests on five practitioner pages and one Google help page, not on a lawyer's view, so have a lawyer read your own contract.

  • Six parts. Domain, hosting, code, content, logins and Google accounts can each have a different owner.
  • The domain first. The registrant of record holds it, and a developer who registered it in their details is that holder until it changes.
  • Code needs paper. Ask for a written assignment or a broad licence; open-source parts stay under their own licences.
  • Handover. Use the register and checklist below on launch day, not after a falling-out.

Who Owns My Website? Six Parts, Often Six Owners

A website is several separate assets, and each can belong to a different person. Violexa counts six (domain, hosting, code, design, content and data), Kamboh Associates six (adding admin login, business email and Google accounts), and Gullysales five. Tanush Digital's handover table runs to over a dozen rows, adding DNS, app stores and the payment gateway (all read on 17 November 2026).

My reading is that the parts fall into three layers: the name (domain and DNS), the place it runs (hosting, code, database, content) and the keys (admin, email, Google, payments, app stores). Lose the name and everything else is hard to use. Lose the keys and you depend on whoever holds them.

Domain Names: You Lease the Name, and the Registrant Holds It

Nobody owns a domain outright: the registrant on record holds the right to use the name and keeps it by renewing. Tanush Digital, an Indian studio, reports that ICANN, which oversees the domain system, compares registering a domain to leasing it. It cites ICANN's FAQ as warning that a developer may register your domain with their own contact details even when you paid, which makes them the registrant of record.

You may then need proof of payment to show the registrar the name should be yours. For .in names, Tanush Digital adds that the registry has no duty to warn you before expiry, so renewing on time is your job.

How to Find Who Owns a Website

To find who owns a website, a public lookup gives you the registrar and expiry date but usually not the person. Tanush Digital says ICANN Lookup or the .IN Registry's WHOIS tends to hide the registrant's name. Gullysales says the registrar can tell the account holder, so you need whoever opened the account.

  1. Look up your domain on ICANN Lookup and note the registrar and expiry date.
  2. Search your inbox for the registrar's name. Kamboh Associates lists renewal requests that come from the developer, not the provider, as a warning sign.
  3. Ask the developer in writing which name and email the account uses.
  4. Put the expiry date in two calendars, as Gullysales advises.

Who Owns My Website Code? Assignment, Licence or Platform

The code belongs to whoever the contract says, and without a written transfer an outside developer may still own what they wrote. Tanush Digital summarises India's Copyright Act this way: computer programmes count as literary works (section 2(o)), and section 17(c) makes an employer the first owner of work made under a contract of service. An outside agency or freelancer is not normally your employee under such a contract.

Violexa, whose examples are Dutch, names three arrangements, which I use as the frame.

ArrangementWhat you getAsk this
AssignmentRights in the custom work pass to youAre rights, period, territory and payment stated?
LicenceYou may use it; the developer stays ownerMay someone else change the code?
PlatformThe site runs on their system or a builderCan the site leave with you?

Tanush Digital says a clause that leaves out the period or territory may give you far less than you think. The Act also allows a written licence in place of an assignment (section 30), which a developer may offer for parts reused across clients. Open-source frameworks stay under their own licences, per Violexa and Tanush Digital, so no developer can assign those to you.

A page on a supplier's site is not a contract, but it shows what a supplier will say plainly. The studio's web development page says it avoids proprietary lock-in and builds for handoff with documentation, and its SaaS page answers "Who owns the code?" with "You do." Ask any supplier for the clause, not the claim.

Code My Own Website: Does Writing It Yourself Settle Ownership?

Coding my own website settles the assignment question for the code I write, in my reading, but not the rest. The domain, hosting and Google accounts still need to be in your name, and any framework or template stays under its own licence. On a website builder, Violexa's platform case applies: the site may not leave with you.

Google Accounts: Search Console, Analytics and Business Profile

You should be the verified owner of your Google accounts and the developer an added user, because Google gives owners the highest permissions. Google's Search Console Help, read on 17 November 2026, says verification lasts only while Search Console can still find your token, and permissions expire after a grace period if it cannot. If all verified owners lose access, all users lose access, and a new owner can unverify previous owners by removing their token.

The table shows what each verification method needs, from that page, with my reading of the ownership trap in each.

Kamboh Associates says owners can add others with limited permissions and remove them later, so nothing needs to be transferred when a project ends. Gullysales suggests two owners on a Business Profile, since one ex-employee's personal account is the same problem as a domain in a developer's name.

Ownership Traps: What Usually Goes Wrong

Most ownership disputes start with a convenience: someone set an account up in their own name to save you the trouble, and nobody changed it. Gullysales says this happens by kindness, not malice, and works until the person moves on. The table pairs each trap with its cause and fix, drawn from the five pages.

TrapHow it happensFix
Domain in their detailsRegistered "for you" in their nameChange the registrant first

Renewals go to themNotices never reach youYour email on every account

Tanush Digital retells three posts by owners in India: one never got the control panel login and says the developer took the site down after a refused demand for extra money for SSL and maintenance; another spent about 4.27 lakh rupees over eight months with an app agency and got no proper documentation; a third describes agencies that bill every change as a change request. These are second-hand anecdotes, not data, but a billing argument becomes a crisis when the other side holds the keys.

What is documented and what is my reading

Documented as of 17 November 2026: Google's rules on Search Console verification, Violexa's three code arrangements, and each page's list of parts and warning signs. The statements about ICANN, the .in registry and India's Copyright Act are as reported by Tanush Digital; I did not read those primary texts.

My reading: the three layers, the register, the trap table and the recovery order. None of the five practitioner pages is independent, since each sells web services.

Agency Lock-In: Seven Signs You Are Stuck

Lock-in means leaving is so hard or costly that you stay by default, and seven signs recur across Violexa and Kamboh Associates. Violexa adds that none is a reason to panic, only a reason to start the conversation and put agreements in writing.

  1. You do not know which company holds your domain or hosting.
  2. You have no login of your own, only a promise that the developer will handle it.
  3. Renewal requests come from the developer, not from the provider.
  4. You have never logged in to your own admin panel.
  5. The developer says the theme or system is theirs and cannot be moved.
  6. You pay for every small change because you cannot edit anything yourself.
  7. Monthly fees continue, but nobody can list what they cover.

My reading: one sign is a question to ask, and three or more is a reason to complete the register this week. Violexa's test is whether you could take the site to another agency: usually yes, if you own the domain, have hosting and code access and the site is not on a closed platform.

Handover Checklist: What You Should Receive at Launch

A proper handover puts every row of the register in your hands before the final payment, and you should test each item by logging in yourself. Violexa advises giving the developer a separate account you can revoke, never your main password.

  • [ ] Domain registered to my business, with auto-renew, two-step sign-in and transfer lock on, and the expiry in two calendars.
  • [ ] DNS and hosting logins in my name, or a written statement of where the site runs plus scheduled full backups.
  • [ ] Source code in a repository my business owns, plus a database and files backup I have downloaded and opened.
  • [ ] An administrator account on the CMS, with the developer on a separate login.
  • [ ] Owner role on Search Console, Analytics and Business Profile, with two Business Profile owners.
  • [ ] Payment gateway, app store and email accounts in my business's name.
  • [ ] A list of paid plugins and services, with cost, renewal date and whose account holds each.
  • [ ] A document listing every item above and how the site is deployed, and a walkthrough of how to edit it.

Contract Clauses: What to Put in Writing

Seven clauses settle most ownership disputes before they begin, and each comes from a named source. They are a checklist for a lawyer, not a contract.

  1. Domain. Registered in your business's name (Violexa).
  2. Code, design and content. Ownership passes on final payment (Kamboh Associates), by written assignment stating rights, period, territory and consideration (Tanush Digital), or a broad licence that lets third parties change the code (Violexa).
  3. Exclusions. Open-source and reused components stay under their own licences, and the contract lists them.
  4. Access. A login per service at delivery, and the code as a download or repository access (Violexa).
  5. Paid licences. Each listed with its cost and whose account holds it.
  6. Recurring fees. A list of what each fee covers (Kamboh Associates).
  7. Exit. A clean handover when the work ends, at a rate agreed in advance (Violexa).

Taking Back Control: Recovery in Order

Recover the domain first, then a copy of the site, then hosting in your name, and switch DNS last. Kamboh Associates, Gullysales and Tanush Digital agree on that order.

  1. Gather proof. Keep every invoice, payment record and email showing you commissioned the site.
  2. Ask in writing. Request each login and the transfer code, citing the agreement, and offer to pay any transfer charge.
  3. Move the registrant first. Tanush Digital says change it before moving registrars. Gullysales warns a registrar may lock a name after a change, so promise no dates.
  4. Get a copy. Ask for a full backup of files and database, or a repository transfer. GitHub keeps the history and adds the original owner as a collaborator, per Tanush Digital.
  5. Host it yourself. Set up hosting in your name and test on a temporary address.
  6. Switch last. Change the domain's settings after the new site works, and keep the old hosting running for some days.
  7. Claim Google. Verify your own ownership in each Google tool.

Here is why the domain comes first. Suppose a lookup on 17 November 2026 shows a .in name expiring on 15 January 2027: 13 days left in November, plus 31 in December, plus 15 in January, is 59 days. Tanush Digital says that once a registrar deletes a .in name, a 30-day Redemption Grace Period follows in which only that registrar can restore it and the site and email stop working, then a 5-day hold, then anyone can register it. If deletion happened on 15 January, the grace period would end about 14 February and the hold about 19 February; the sources do not say how soon after expiry deletion happens, so that date is my assumption.

If the other party will not cooperate, Tanush Digital notes that dispute processes such as the INDRP exist for .in domains, and Gullysales says to take legal advice before paying anyone to release what you believe is yours. The last resort is a rebuild from copies of the live site: the pricing page explains how Elyx quotes, and a business website usually takes 2–4 weeks, with engagements starting from ₹9,999, excluding GST.

What to Do Next

Spend five minutes today on the domain lookup, then an hour this week filling in the register. Log in to every account yourself, write "not mine" beside any row you cannot open, and send the developer one friendly written request for those items while the relationship is still good.

Frequently asked questions

Who owns my website?

You own the parts that are registered or assigned to you, and the developer or host owns the rest. Violexa says that if the domain, code and access are not all sorted, you depend on your developer even after paying every invoice. Check the register above row by row.

Who owns my website domain?

The registrant of record holds the right to use it, which is whoever's details the registrar has. Tanush Digital says a developer who registered it with their own details is the holder, even if you paid. Ask the registrar for the account holder and change the registrant to your business.

How do I check who owns a website?

Run a lookup such as ICANN Lookup, which shows the registrar and expiry date. Tanush Digital says it usually hides the registrant's name, so ask the registrar.

Who owns my website code if an agency built it?

It depends on the contract, and without a written transfer the agency may still own it. Tanush Digital's reading of India's Copyright Act is that an outside developer is not normally your employee under a contract of service. Ask for a written assignment or a broad licence, and have a lawyer check it.

Can my web developer refuse to give me the logins?

It depends on what you agreed, but refusing access to your own domain or admin area is a bad sign. Violexa advises raising it, putting the agreement in writing and taking legal advice in a dispute.

Can I take my website to another agency?

Usually yes, if you own the domain, have hosting and code access and the site is not on a closed platform. Violexa says a closed platform often means a rebuild. Move the accounts and copies first, and switch the domain last.

Can I get a lapsed .in domain back?

Possibly, if you act fast. Tanush Digital says a deleted .in domain enters a 30-day Redemption Grace Period in which only the registrar that held it can restore it, followed by a 5-day hold. Contact that registrar immediately.

Can I code my own website and own all of it?

You can own the code you write, but the domain, hosting and accounts still need to be in your name. Frameworks and templates stay under their own licences, and a website builder may keep the site on its platform.
