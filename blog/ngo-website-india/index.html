NGO and Trust Websites That Raise Donations: 80G, Payments and Transparency

NGO website India checklist: what a donation page, 80G block, impact report and payment setup need, read from four large NGO sites and one audit list.

An NGO website in India raises donations when a stranger can check who you are, see where the money goes and give by UPI or card in under a minute. That takes four parts: a donation page, a visible 80G and registration block, a dated impact report and a payment setup that issues a receipt. The qualification that matters is regulation: the research behind this article holds no primary tax text, so the 80G and payment sections are a business checklist, not tax or legal advice.

  • Donation page. One page that explains how to give, with receipt terms stated before the donor pays.
  • 80G and registration. State your status, its dates and its scope, and have a chartered accountant confirm each line.
  • Impact reports. Every number needs a year, a place and a method.
  • Payments. Offer UPI and cards beside net banking, and ask the gateway about fees and refunds.

Donation Pages: One Page, Eight Parts

A donation page should explain how to give and nothing else, and it should carry eight parts a donor checks before paying. The audit checklist from the studio digiSarathi, which advises Indian NGOs, lists them under fundraising readiness.

  • Purpose. A page whose whole job is to explain how someone gives money.
  • Methods. UPI and card beside net banking, not bank transfer only.
  • Use of funds. A breakdown of what a gift pays for, with honest overhead disclosure.
  • Receipt terms. What the donor receives and how long after giving.
  • 80G. The benefit explained if it applies, and whether it covers all giving.
  • Partnerships. A route for a corporate or CSR manager.
  • Profile. A one-page organisation profile, current within the year.
  • Contact. A named, monitored email for funding enquiries, with a response time you can keep.

My reading adds two things the sources do not test. Offer a monthly option, since CRY lists monthly donors as a way to give, and put receipt terms above the pay button, not in a footer.

Worked Example: What a Monthly Option Changes

These numbers are assumptions to replace with your own, not benchmarks. Suppose 2,000 people visit the page in a month and 3 percent give: 2,000 × 0.03 = 60 gifts. At an average gift of 1,000 rupees, that is 60 × 1,000 = 60,000 rupees.

Now suppose 10 of the 60 donors choose 500 rupees a month instead. Over twelve months those ten give 10 × 500 × 12 = 60,000 rupees, against 10 × 1,000 = 10,000 rupees as one-off gifts. That holds only if they all stay the year, which a site cannot promise.

80G Receipts: What the Page Should State and What to Confirm

An NGO website should state plainly whether 80G applies, to which gifts and when the receipt arrives, and a chartered accountant should confirm each statement before it goes live. Section 80G is the income tax provision that lets some donors deduct part of a gift.

Documented. As of 16 November 2026, Bal Raksha Bharat's own site says all donations to it are 50% tax-exempt under section 80G of the Income Tax Act 1961. That is the organisation's claim about itself, which we did not check against the Act. The digiSarathi checklist tells NGOs to explain the 80G benefit, say whether it covers all giving, and show 12A and 12AB status with the dates each is valid.

What the sources do not cover. Our package holds no text of the Income Tax Act, no current deduction rates, no rule on validity or renewal and no receipt format. Do not copy another NGO's 80G wording. Ask your chartered accountant, and check the Income Tax Department's own pages, then date the line on your page so the next editor knows when it was last confirmed.

Payment Gateways and UPI for Donations

A donation page should offer UPI and cards beside net banking through a payment gateway that issues a receipt, and the gateway's own terms decide fees and onboarding. The digiSarathi checklist asks for UPI and card options alongside net banking, plus bank details with the current IFSC. CRY lists cards, net banking, wallets, UPI, card-points redemption, monthly giving and legacy giving.

The studio's coaching-centre page says it can add UPI and card payments through a gateway, plus receipts. Our sources name no gateway, fee, settlement time or onboarding document. The RBI circular in the research, dated 12 May 2016, covers offices of foreign entities, not payment gateways, and the NPCI page returned a 404 error. So ask each gateway in writing:

  • Does it onboard your legal form, and which documents does it want?
  • What does it charge, and when does money reach your account?
  • How are refunds, failed payments and receipts handled?
  • Does it support monthly giving, and how does the donor consent?

Impact Reports: Dated Numbers a Funder Can Check

An impact report works for a funder when every number carries its year, its place and the way it was measured. The digiSarathi checklist asks for the current financial year's annual report as a downloadable PDF and also readable on the page, audited statements for the last two years, and an honest note where outcomes are not yet measured.

Real home pages show the difference. The table reads three of them as of 16 November 2026, quoting each figure as the site gives it.

SiteFigure as statedYear stamped?
CRYOver 23 lakh children reachedYes, 2025-26
Bal Raksha Bharat13.8 lakh children across 15 statesYes, April 2022 to March 2023
Smile FoundationOver 20 lakh, and in another place over 15 lakh, children a yearNo

The Bal Raksha figures are dated, but they are three years old on a page that also has an Annual Report 2026 section. Smile Foundation's extracted text carries two versions of one sentence with different numbers and no year. A donor or a CSR reviewer cannot tell which is current, and that is the mistake to avoid.

Copy this template for each programme page:

  • Programme: name and goal.
  • Period: April 20__ to March 20__.
  • Place: named districts or blocks, not "across India".
  • Output: households, children or litres, with the number.
  • Outcome: the result, or "not yet measured".
  • Method: how you count it.
  • Source: a link to the report or evaluation.

Registered NGO in India: The Compliance Block

A registered NGO in India should show its legal name, registration number, tax status and any foreign-funding registration in one block a visitor finds without searching. The digiSarathi checklist lists the contents.

  • Legal identity. Full registered name exactly as on the certificate, the registration number and the state authority.
  • Tax status. 12A and 12AB, with validity dates.
  • Foreign funds. FCRA number and validity, if you receive foreign contributions.
  • Listings. Your Darpan listing, linked, plus GuideStar India or similar registries.
  • People. A named founder or director, and the year founded and registered.
AreaMinimumStrong
IdentityName and number on the About pageFull block with 12A, 12AB and FCRA, linked to certificates
GovernanceTrustee namesRoles, meeting dates, conflict of interest policy
GivingBank transfer instructionsUPI, card and transfer, with use of funds and receipt terms

The table condenses digiSarathi's own minimum-versus-strong grid. Smile Foundation shows how third parties add weight: its page lists GuideStar India Platinum, CAF America and GlobalGiving validation. For the same logic on a clinic's registration number, see the website for doctors in India article.

Our sources do not explain how to register an NGO online or choose between a trust, a society and a company, so this article does not. Ask a chartered accountant or lawyer, because the site follows registration, never replaces it. All NGO India's community guidelines also tell contributors not to submit information they know is false, outdated or misleading about registration, funding or impact.

For an international NGO, the RBI circular of 12 May 2016 says a non-profit applying to open a branch, liaison or project office needs the Reserve Bank's prior approval, routed through an authorised dealer bank. The text we received is trimmed and over ten years old, so confirm current rules, and state on your India page which Indian entity receives funds.

CSR Funders: What They Check Before They Write Back

A CSR or partnerships manager looks for proof they can forward internally: registration, a current report, a named contact and a partnerships page. The digiSarathi checklist opens with an NGO whose site had run four years, whose annual report link pointed two financial years back and whose CSR enquiry waited three weeks for a certificate. That is one practitioner's account, not a measured rate.

Smile Foundation's page lists CSR awards that name its corporate partner, and Bal Raksha Bharat has a corporate partnership section and an article on CSR versus personal donation. My reading is that CSR funding is rechecked each year, so a current site earns the next round and a stale one loses it. Our sources do not cover CSR law or eligibility, so confirm those with the funder.

A CSR pack, ready to send, holds:

  • The registration and tax-status block with dates.
  • The current annual report and two years of audited statements.
  • The one-page profile.
  • One programme page with a dated, sourced result.
  • A named contact and a reply time.

Mobile, Language and Accessibility

Donors will mostly meet your site on a phone, so it must load on an entry-level Android over a weak connection and read without zooming. The digiSarathi checklist asks for 16px body text, enough contrast, alt text on images, no text inside images, a visible WhatsApp or phone option and the languages your donors use. The studio's web page says it builds mobile-first and adds a CMS, so your own team can edit the report link and the 80G line.

What to Do Next: A Seven-Step Plan

Start with the compliance block and the donation page, then the report and the partnerships page. Work through this checklist:

  • [ ] I named one person who owns the website.
  • [ ] My compliance block shows name, number, 12A, 12AB, FCRA and Darpan, with dates.
  • [ ] My 80G line was checked by a chartered accountant and carries a date.
  • [ ] My donation page states UPI, card and net banking, plus receipt terms.
  • [ ] I asked a gateway in writing about onboarding, fees, refunds and monthly giving.
  • [ ] Each programme page has a year, a place, a number and a method.
  • [ ] I can send a CSR pack in one email.

Frequently asked questions

What is an NGO in India?

An NGO is a non-governmental organisation: a group that works on a social cause without being part of government. Our sources do not define the legal forms, so confirm with a chartered accountant whether a trust, society or company suits you.

How do I register an NGO in India?

Registration is done with the state or company authority for the legal form you choose, and our sources do not describe the steps. Ask a chartered accountant or lawyer. Once registered, your website should show the name, number and tax status.

What should an NGO website in India include?

An NGO website in India should include a compliance block, a donation page, programme pages with dated numbers, the current annual report, a partnerships page and a named contact. The digiSarathi checklist covers each. Add a visible WhatsApp or phone option.

How do I show 80G on an NGO website?

State whether 80G applies, which gifts it covers and when the receipt arrives, and date the line. Bal Raksha Bharat, for example, states a 50% figure under section 80G on its own site. Have a chartered accountant confirm your wording first.

Can an NGO website accept UPI donations?

Yes, a payment gateway can add UPI and card payments with receipts, and the studio says it builds this. CRY lists UPI among its giving options. Ask the gateway whether it onboards your legal form, and what it charges.

How often should an NGO update its impact numbers?

Update them with every financial year, and check the page quarterly. The digiSarathi checklist wants the current year's annual report, not last year's. Stamp each figure with its period, as CRY does with 2025-26.

What do CSR managers look for on an NGO website?

They look for registration and tax status, a current report, a partnerships page and a named contact who replies. The digiSarathi checklist lists all four. Our sources do not cover CSR eligibility rules, so confirm those with the funder.

How much does an NGO website cost in India?

An NGO website's indicative range is ₹9,999–₹24,999 for a landing page or ₹24,999–₹59,999 for a business site, excluding 18% GST. Online payments move the price. These are draft ranges, never a quote, and a fixed quote is confirmed in writing after a call.
